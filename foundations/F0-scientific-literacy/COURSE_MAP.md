# F0 — COURSE MAP

**Production state:** `ARCHITECTED`
**Scope:** F0.1–F0.10 — scientific literacy and quantitative reasoning
**Purpose:** build the methodological competence required to evaluate nutrition and exercise evidence before domain-specific claims are taught.

## 1. Exit competency

At the end of F0, the learner must be able to take a complete nutrition/exercise paper and, without relying on a checklist as a substitute for reasoning:

1. reconstruct the scientific question and the estimand/target quantity of interest;
2. identify what the study design can and cannot support;
3. identify major threats from bias, confounding, selection, measurement and missing data;
4. interpret descriptive statistics, effect estimates, uncertainty and basic inferential outputs;
5. distinguish statistical compatibility/significance from magnitude and practical importance;
6. reason about Type I/II error, power, precision, multiplicity and analytical flexibility;
7. interpret a systematic review/meta-analysis, including heterogeneity and limits of pooling;
8. distinguish reporting quality, risk of bias, certainty of a body of evidence and applicability;
9. produce a defensible critical appraisal of a full paper;
10. communicate conclusions with uncertainty proportional to the evidence.

The target is not memorization of terminology. The learner must demonstrate `K1–K8` competencies from the mastery protocol, with emphasis on `K4` interpretation of evidence, `K6` application/calculation, `K7` integration and `K8` limits/uncertainty.

## 2. Instructional principles

Every unit follows, when applicable:

`accessible language → formal concept → worked example → counterexample → technical language → evidence/method standard → critical task → active recall → transfer to sports nutrition`

A reporting guideline is never taught as a quality score. CONSORT, STROBE and PRISMA are used to understand what transparent reporting should expose; risk-of-bias tools and causal reasoning are taught separately.

All numerical examples should preferentially use exercise, nutrition or human-performance contexts, while avoiding premature teaching of substantive nutrition claims.

## 3. Prerequisite classes used in F0

- `P0` — local definition that can be introduced at point of use.
- `P1` — brief support concept that needs an embedded subsection/worked example.
- `P2` — structural dependency that must be learned before the target competency.

### Conditional quantitative bridge

Before F0.4, run a short diagnostic on:

- fractions, ratios and percentages;
- percentage versus percentage-point change;
- simple algebraic rearrangement;
- scientific notation;
- reading axes, tables and basic plots;
- mean/median intuition;
- probability from 0 to 1.

If only isolated gaps appear, treat them as `P1` and repair them inside F0.4. If the learner cannot reliably perform the majority of these operations, promote the bridge to `P2-QB` and complete it before continuing to F0.4. This prevents hidden numeracy gaps from contaminating later inference modules.

## 4. Unit map

### F0.1 — Scientific questions, hypotheses and operationalization

**Competency:** convert a vague claim into a precise, answerable scientific question and identify the quantity the study is actually trying to estimate.

**Learning questions**

- What makes a question empirically answerable?
- How do population, exposure/intervention, comparator, outcome and time frame constrain the claim?
- What is the difference between construct, operational definition, variable, endpoint and estimand?
- What is the difference between exploratory and confirmatory reasoning?
- How can a hypothesis be falsifiable yet still poorly operationalized?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: population, sample, variable, exposure, intervention, comparator, outcome.
- `P1`: measurement scale and time horizon.
- `P2`: none.

**Required content**

- question → target population → contrast → outcome → time → estimand;
- directional/non-directional hypotheses;
- construct validity and operationalization;
- primary versus secondary/exploratory outcomes;
- prespecification versus post hoc analysis;
- why a precise question constrains valid interpretation.

**Core resources:** `F0-S01`, `F0-S05`, `F0-S14`, `F0-S15`.

**Performance task:** rewrite five popular nutrition/performance claims into answerable questions; identify what evidence would count against each claim and what conclusion would still remain unsupported.

**Handoff:** F0.1 supplies the question/estimand that determines which study design in F0.2 is fit for purpose.

---

### F0.2 — Study designs and what they can answer

**Competency:** identify common human-research designs and reason from design features to the class of inference they can support.

**Learning questions**

- What does random allocation buy us, and what does it not guarantee?
- How do parallel, crossover, cluster and factorial trials differ?
- What can cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies establish?
- When are repeated measures, acute laboratory studies and longitudinal studies informative?
- Why is “RCT > observational” an inadequate universal ranking?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: allocation, follow-up, exposure, outcome, prospective/retrospective.
- `P1`: within-subject versus between-subject comparison; washout/carryover intuition.
- `P2`: F0.1.

**Required content**

- randomized trials: parallel, crossover, cluster, factorial;
- non-randomized intervention studies;
- cohort, case-control, cross-sectional designs;
- acute mechanistic versus chronic outcome studies;
- diagnostic/prognostic questions as distinct design problems;
- unit of allocation versus unit of analysis;
- temporal ordering;
- target trial concept as a causal-design benchmark for observational intervention questions.

**Core resources:** `F0-S01`, `F0-S02`, `F0-S03`, `F0-S14`, `F0-S16`.

**Performance task:** given eight short study descriptions, classify the design, state the strongest defensible inference and name one tempting but invalid inference.

**Handoff:** F0.2 provides the design skeleton needed to understand bias and causal identification in F0.3.

---

### F0.3 — Bias, confounding, causal reasoning and validity

**Competency:** identify major pathways by which an observed association/effect estimate can differ from the causal effect or target quantity of interest.

**Learning questions**

- What is bias, and how is it different from random error?
- What is confounding, and why can adjustment both help and harm?
- What are selection bias, information/measurement bias and missing-data bias?
- Why does randomization reduce some causal problems but not every threat to validity?
- What are internal validity, external validity and transportability/applicability?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: confounder, mediator, collider, selection, measurement error, validity.
- `P1`: counterfactual intuition; simple directed graphs.
- `P2`: F0.1 and F0.2.

**Required content**

- systematic versus random error;
- confounding and residual confounding;
- selection mechanisms;
- measurement and misclassification;
- attrition/missingness as a source of bias;
- reverse causation;
- collider bias at conceptual level;
- causal diagrams as reasoning aids, not proof generators;
- target-trial thinking;
- internal versus external validity.

**Core resources:** `F0-S07`, `F0-S11`, `F0-S14`, `F0-S16`, `F0-S17`.

**Performance task:** annotate a simplified causal diagram for a supplement–performance question, identify at least two invalid adjustment strategies and explain how they could distort the estimate.

**Handoff:** F0.3 establishes systematic error; F0.4–F0.6 add random error and quantitative inference.

---

### F0.4 — Descriptive statistics, distributions and sampling variation

**Competency:** summarize data appropriately and understand how distributions and sampling variation shape interpretation.

**Learning questions**

- What do mean, median, SD, IQR and percentiles actually describe?
- How do skew, outliers and bounded variables affect summaries?
- What is the difference between a sample distribution and a sampling distribution?
- Why is standard deviation not standard error?
- How can graphs reveal information hidden by a single summary statistic?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: observation, variable type, center, spread, distribution.
- `P1`: quantitative bridge items when isolated gaps are present.
- `P2`: F0.1; conditional `P2-QB` if the numeracy diagnostic fails.

**Required content**

- variable types and measurement scales;
- mean, median, mode, range, variance, SD, IQR, quantiles;
- distributions and skew;
- outliers and data visualization;
- sample versus population;
- sampling variability;
- standard deviation versus standard error;
- transformations only at conceptual level unless needed for later examples.

**Core resources:** `F0-S12`, `F0-S14`.

**Performance task:** choose and justify summaries for four datasets, detect misleading graph choices, and explain what changes when sample size increases while variability stays similar.

**Handoff:** F0.4 supplies the quantitative objects used in estimation and inferential reasoning in F0.5.

---

### F0.5 — Estimates, confidence intervals, p-values, effect sizes and practical relevance

**Competency:** interpret point estimates and uncertainty without dichotomous-significance reasoning.

**Learning questions**

- What is a point estimate and what is an interval estimate?
- What does a frequentist confidence interval mean—and not mean?
- What does a p-value mean—and not mean?
- Why are magnitude and precision more informative than a significance label alone?
- How do absolute and relative effects differ?
- What makes an effect practically meaningful in sports/nutrition contexts?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: parameter, estimate, null hypothesis, standard error.
- `P1`: ratios and logarithmic scales for relative measures when introduced.
- `P2`: F0.4.

**Required content**

- point estimates and confidence intervals;
- frequentist interpretation of confidence level;
- null-hypothesis significance testing;
- six core ASA cautions around p-values;
- standardized versus raw effect sizes;
- mean difference, standardized mean difference, risk difference, risk ratio and odds ratio at conceptual/applied level;
- absolute versus relative effects;
- smallest effect of interest / decision threshold concept;
- statistical versus practical importance;
- compatibility of multiple effect sizes with the data.

**Core resources:** `F0-S06`, `F0-S08`, `F0-S09`, `F0-S12`, `F0-S13`.

**Performance task:** interpret a forest-plot row and a small trial table in plain language, explicitly separating direction, magnitude, precision, statistical output and practical importance.

**Handoff:** F0.5 makes uncertainty visible; F0.6 explains how design and analytical choices alter error rates and informativeness.

---

### F0.6 — Power, Type I/II error, multiplicity and analytical flexibility

**Competency:** evaluate whether a study was capable of producing an informative answer and recognize how multiple testing/flexible analysis can inflate false conclusions.

**Learning questions**

- What are Type I and Type II errors in repeated-use terms?
- What is statistical power conditional on an assumed effect and design?
- Why is post hoc “observed power” usually unhelpful?
- How can multiplicity arise from endpoints, time points, subgroups, models and stopping rules?
- Why do preregistration, protocols and statistical analysis plans matter?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: alpha, beta, power, family of hypotheses, prespecification.
- `P1`: probability complements; simple power-curve interpretation.
- `P2`: F0.4 and F0.5.

**Required content**

- Type I/II error and power;
- power versus precision;
- sample-size justification and smallest effect of interest;
- multiplicity and family-wise false-positive risk;
- endpoint hierarchies and adjustment concepts;
- subgroup and secondary analyses;
- researcher degrees of freedom / analytical flexibility;
- preregistration, protocol and statistical analysis plan;
- distinction between confirmatory and exploratory outputs.

**Core resources:** `F0-S01`, `F0-S08`, `F0-S09`, `F0-S10`, `F0-S13`.

**Performance task:** audit an imaginary trial with 20 outcomes and several time points; identify the multiplicity problem, distinguish planned from exploratory analyses and propose a transparent reporting strategy.

**Handoff:** F0.6 is required to judge both individual studies and meta-analytic bodies of literature in F0.7.

---

### F0.7 — Systematic reviews, meta-analyses and heterogeneity

**Competency:** interpret evidence synthesis without treating a pooled estimate as automatically superior or homogeneous truth.

**Learning questions**

- What makes a review systematic?
- What problems do eligibility criteria, searching and selective inclusion create?
- What is a meta-analysis actually pooling?
- What does heterogeneity mean conceptually and statistically?
- When should studies not be pooled?
- What do subgroup/sensitivity analyses and publication bias add—or fail to add?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: eligibility criteria, effect measure, weight, pooled estimate, heterogeneity.
- `P1`: inverse-variance intuition; log scale for ratio measures; forest-plot anatomy.
- `P2`: F0.2, F0.5 and F0.6; F0.3 strongly recommended before risk-of-bias synthesis.

**Required content**

- systematic review workflow;
- protocol/question/eligibility/search/extraction;
- effect measure compatibility;
- fixed-effect versus random-effects conceptual distinction;
- forest plots;
- statistical and clinical/methodological heterogeneity;
- I² interpreted cautiously, not as a quality score;
- sensitivity/subgroup/meta-regression concepts;
- small-study effects/publication bias at introductory level;
- why PRISMA is a reporting guideline, not a risk-of-bias score.

**Core resources:** `F0-S04`, `F0-S05`, `F0-S06`.

**Performance task:** interpret a forest plot containing heterogeneous trials and produce two alternative explanations for the heterogeneity before accepting a subgroup story.

**Handoff:** F0.7 introduces bodies of evidence; F0.8 asks how much confidence to place in them and whether they apply to the target context.

---

### F0.8 — Risk of bias, certainty of evidence and applicability

**Competency:** separately judge (a) bias within studies, (b) certainty across a body of evidence, and (c) directness/applicability to the question of interest.

**Learning questions**

- Why is reporting completeness not the same thing as low risk of bias?
- How does outcome-specific risk of bias differ from a global “study quality” score?
- How do RoB 2 and ROBINS-I structure judgments?
- What does GRADE mean by certainty of evidence?
- How do risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and dissemination/publication bias affect confidence?
- How does applicability depend on population, intervention, comparator, outcome and context?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: domain-based judgment, certainty, directness, threshold.
- `P1`: PICO comparison across evidence and target question.
- `P2`: F0.2, F0.3, F0.5 and F0.7.

**Required content**

- reporting guideline versus critical appraisal versus risk-of-bias tool;
- RoB 2 domains at conceptual level;
- ROBINS-I/target-trial logic at conceptual level;
- outcome-specific judgments;
- GRADE certainty categories;
- risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and missing/dissemination bias;
- decision thresholds / practical importance;
- applicability/generalizability/transportability;
- certainty is a property of a body of evidence for an outcome/question, not a prestige label for a paper.

**Core resources:** `F0-S02`, `F0-S03`, `F0-S05`, `F0-S07`, `F0-S11`, `F0-S18`, `F0-S19`, `F0-S20`.

**Performance task:** compare two evidence bodies that have similar pooled effects but different certainty/applicability and defend why recommendations should not be equally confident.

**Handoff:** F0.8 provides the appraisal framework used in the full-paper practicum F0.9.

---

### F0.9 — Critical reading of a complete paper

**Competency:** perform a structured, evidence-aware appraisal of a full paper from question through conclusion.

**Learning questions**

- Can the stated conclusion be reconstructed from the methods and results?
- Where do protocol, registration and reporting choices change confidence?
- Which limitations materially threaten the target inference?
- Are the authors discussing the estimate they actually measured?
- Which findings are primary, secondary, exploratory or post hoc?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: article anatomy and supplementary-material navigation.
- `P1`: extracting a PICO/estimand table and a result table.
- `P2`: F0.1–F0.8.

**Required content**

A fixed appraisal sequence:

1. question and estimand;
2. design and sampling;
3. intervention/exposure and comparator;
4. outcome measurement;
5. bias/confounding/missingness;
6. sample size and analysis plan;
7. effect estimate and uncertainty;
8. multiplicity/exploration;
9. result robustness;
10. applicability;
11. consistency with authors’ conclusion;
12. what the paper does not establish.

Reporting guidelines may be used to locate missing information but must not replace reasoning about validity.

**Core resources:** all prior F0 core resources, especially `F0-S01`, `F0-S02`, `F0-S03`, `F0-S07`, `F0-S18`, `F0-S19`.

**Performance task:** complete a blind critical appraisal of one full open-access exercise/nutrition paper, then compare the appraisal against a structured answer key.

**Handoff:** F0.9 produces the evidence judgment that must be communicated responsibly in F0.10.

---

### F0.10 — Synthesis and communication of uncertainty

**Competency:** communicate an evidence-based conclusion without overstating certainty, hiding limitations or collapsing a distribution of plausible effects into a binary claim.

**Learning questions**

- How should strength of language track certainty?
- How do we distinguish “no evidence of effect” from “evidence of no meaningful effect”?
- How should magnitude, precision and practical threshold be communicated together?
- How do we report conflicting studies without false balance?
- When is a recommendation stronger or weaker than the certainty of one outcome alone?

**Prerequisites**

- `P0`: calibrated language, claim scope, uncertainty statement.
- `P1`: concise plain-language synthesis.
- `P2`: F0.5, F0.8 and F0.9; F0.7 for multi-study synthesis.

**Required content**

- calibrated verbs and certainty labels;
- point estimate + interval + practical threshold;
- absence of evidence versus evidence compatible with trivial effects;
- conflict between sources and weight of evidence;
- direct versus indirect evidence;
- uncertainty caused by bias versus imprecision versus indirectness;
- recommendation versus evidence statement;
- explicit statement of what would change the conclusion.

**Core resources:** `F0-S08`, `F0-S09`, `F0-S18`, `F0-S19`, `F0-S20`.

**Performance task:** produce three versions of the same evidence synthesis: technical, practitioner-facing and lay-facing, preserving the same epistemic content.

**Handoff:** successful F0.10 plus the exit assessment unlocks F1 content production/study without removing the need for later cumulative F0 retesting.

## 5. Spiral reinforcement after F0

F0 competencies must recur throughout the curriculum:

- F1–F3: distinguish mechanism from demonstrated human outcome;
- D1–D8: interpret domain-specific RCTs, observational studies and meta-analyses;
- A1–A9: apply external validity and decision thresholds to athlete scenarios;
- C1: explicitly search for conflicting evidence and analytic flexibility;
- X1: reconstruct consensus, update claims and defend conclusions.

F0 is therefore a gateway, not a one-time methods block.
